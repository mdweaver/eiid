# Direction review of eiPartialID

**Batch F · 18 November 2026 · [mdweaver/eiid at 27557df](https://github.com/mdweaver/eiid/tree/27557df4747536a9efea60928787871d2d465817/docs/extension)**

## Summary

**Choose M-SHARP before M-LOC, with a narrower first release.** Build an exact population benchmark for two rows or two columns, then implement an explicitly outer confidence procedure for sparse designs. This would answer a question users can act on: how much does the observed distribution of returns tell us beyond its fitted regression, under the same behavioural assumptions? In a 2×3 table, conditional cell limits and adding-up suffice for that benchmark. In a 2×4 table they can miss an entire restriction, even with the conditional law known exactly.

My three principal disagreements are these.

1. **Lead with the quantity the application asks about.** For “What share of these voters stayed loyal?”, print the realised-electorate bound first. A bound for the expected rate at these precinct compositions answers a second question. Full-law M-SHARP improves the information used to constrain that expectation. Realised, model-free Duncan–Davis (DD) endpoints already exploit every supplied precinct margin.
2. **Keep the skew-symmetric parameterisation as the LCE solver. Give the package a more general foundation in compatible tables and conditional means.** This would make the existing realised-sign theory, external counts, and nonparametric shape restrictions first-class options. It would also prevent sharpness for one information set from being inherited by modules that use another.
3. **Make the coverage claim determine the default interval.** Lead with projection where its assumptions and dimension regime apply. For many small districts, lead with finite-sample guarantees and the arithmetic bound. Show what the algorithmic CLR interval buys in width alongside the applicable benchmark.

Three research investments have the best immediate return. **Subset inequalities** give an explicit finite family of sharp conditional constraints for 2×C and R×2. **Targeted validation** tells a researcher which additional precinct cross-tab to obtain, with an exact advance calculation of the resulting reduction in DD width. **Finite composition contrasts** extend the ordering/local-context sandwich to observed pairs without requiring a derivative or a straight line. The first is a computational advance on Avenue F, the second changes the design of data collection in Avenue E, and the third supplies a substantively different shape restriction.

The recommendations below take the pinned results as inputs. New identities have short derivations. Claims about empirical gains are conditional or conjectural. The independent numerical check in Part II concerns one proposed identity only.

### 1. The estimand should follow the question

**EI perspective.** A historian asking how many veterans voted yes in these townships wants a count, or a count divided by the number of eligible veterans. For that question, sum over the same eligible men in both margins. With exact margins, DD bounds the actual count in this election by arithmetic. Report that range and, when requested, the realised-sign restriction imposed directly on possible allocations. For a model-based interval for the same rate, write out the additional coverage claim: across repeated precinct outcomes with these sizes, compositions and district effects held fixed, the procedure covers the realised rate with the stated probability. Intersect a valid interval for this rate with its exact DD range. The latter always contains the target.

A second user might ask how large the gap is **on average in precincts with this design**. Here the conditional-mean target $\tilde B$ is primary. PHASE3 §0.2 already distinguishes this from both $B$ and the superpopulation ratio $B^*$. Keep $B^*$ in the theory. A confidence statement for new, randomly sampled districts would require a sampling law and a different treatment of district variation. D3 is coherent for the first two questions. No change in clustering policy is needed to make their answers legible.

### 2. Keep two representations in the public interface

**Partial-identification perspective.** For the realised population, let $n_{irc}$ be the unknown cell counts. Fix their observed margins, add any known cells or published totals, and optimise the requested functional. For conditional means, the primitive object at design value $v$ is the set of attainable means of a table drawn from the observed conditional distribution. A restriction can act on a realised allocation, on its conditional expectation, or on the link between two of them. The LCE claim $m(x)=(Q+K)x$ gives a compact parametrisation of the last problem. PHASE2a Prop. 7 and PHASE5a §1.2 justify keeping that solver.

A genuinely nonparametric mean model assigns one vector of unknown means to each supported design value. At a finite set of replicated types, intersect the conditional feasible sets with cross-type ordering or finite-difference rows; optimise their design-weighted sum. With many unique types, unrestricted pointwise means cannot be estimated precisely from one return apiece. Aggregate prespecified moment inequalities over supported regions, or posit and disclose smoothness. Evaluating a curve between isolated types is a further restriction. With no cross-type restrictions, much of the parameter space factorises and should be left that way.

Have each module declare its variables, target, conditioning variables, information used, and whether it returns a sharp set or a relaxation. Intersections of projected scalar ranges may discard the requirement that both endpoints have a common witnessing table. Where layers share latent tables, enforce both on those tables before projection. When combining probabilistic statements, specify their joint coverage event. These changes generalise Design §1.2 without replacing the LCE code.

### 3. Specify what M-SHARP makes sharp

**Mathematical perspective.** PHASE2a §4.3 distinguishes $\Theta_I\subseteq\Theta_X\subseteq\Theta_O$. The first uses the conditional law given $(X,N)$ and the model's size-invariant mean. The middle set pools over sizes at a given composition. The last uses the regression and probability validity. PHASE2a Prop. 2 makes the last set sharp over laws with the same regression and design. Prop. 3 gives circumstances in which the extra distributional information buys nothing. PHASE5a §1.2 extends the information distinction to admissible sign restrictions. Both the Draft Design L0+F label and NEXT_BUILD item 4 obscure these distinctions.

A simple separation is informative. At $x=1/2$, let small and large precincts occur equally often. In small precincts $T$ equals 0 or 1 with equal probability. In large precincts $T=1/2$ surely. Both types have the same aggregate mean. The small precincts force the conditional mean rate of each group to equal 1/2. Size invariance transfers this to large precincts. Pooling over size yields the interval $[1/4,3/4]$ for the first group at this composition. The example describes one support point; other compositions can supply regression rank. A size adjustment to the *mean regression* alone cannot recover the discarded information. Conversely, pooling size strata can be a deliberate outer relaxation when their separate laws are too noisy. Label that choice explicitly.

Start with an oracle that takes the law at each replicated design type as known. Then show the estimated confidence region using a finite family of support inequalities. On scattered designs, a finite moment family gives a valid outer procedure under stated conditions. A fine grid, series fit, or population inclusion does not guarantee that estimated intervals will be narrower after accounting for uncertainty. Give the proposed series estimator a comparator and measure incremental gain in both population width and confidence width. Remove “tightens every rung” as a release criterion.

### 4. Let the inference regime choose the default

For pooled fixed-dimensional LCE, and for fixed numbers of districts with increasing within-district information, make the applicable coefficient-projection interval the headline model-based result, subject to its rank, variance and design conditions. Preserve branch unions when a sign disjunction yields a gap. The headline should say whether coverage concerns a scalar true value, a whole identified set, or multiple targets simultaneously. PHASE5a §3.2(c) gives a true-branch argument for scalar coverage. It does not license selecting whichever assumption or endpoint looks most favourable after inspecting the data.

For increasing numbers of small districts, finite-sample inference in PHASE3 §5.1 can be wide. Print the actual width next to the exact DD width and the effective number of units for the focal group, $1/\sum_i w_{ir}^2$. In this regime the grouping structure can consume the information needed to estimate its own effects. Keep the finite-sample result available even when the two widths coincide. Simulation can compare procedures on fixed designs with weak overlap, near ties, a few influential units and wrong signs. Report false exclusion as well as width, stratified by regime. For example, replace the Guide’s assurance that a confidence interval fixes the plug-in problem with a statement a reader can check: “This interval covers the expected loyalty rate at the observed precinct compositions under independent precinct residuals and a fixed number of adequately sampled districts.” Give the realised-rate interval its own description. Conditioning on district effects leaves conditional unit independence as a substantive requirement. If neighbourhood dependence is credible, the assumed coverage has no established justification for that application.

### 5. Replace the master ladder with short, proved chains

The parameter $\delta$ in a fixed district model yields a genuine nesting. A positive between-group association and a positive within-group association are distinct beliefs; neither entails the other. Unknown-sign contextual reinforcement (CR) allows an all-negative branch. Positive between and positive within together imply CR, while CR alone need not imply either positive sign. The arrow in Design §4.2 should refer to a **pair of signed beliefs** if that is its premise. Likewise, unit ordering and smoothness express different substantive commitments. A common-index assumption can be highly credible for one ballot and implausible for another. Give users a graph of proved implications within the same object and design. Put elicited comparisons beside that graph; numbers such as T1.1–T1.6 currently suggest a universal ranking.

Ask the user whether their claim is about the people who cast ballots or the expected rates across comparable precincts. In a model-free analysis use the former sign in PHASE2c M2. Under LCE use the latter in M5. For three groups, naming the pair that exchanges share, or explicitly pooling the remaining groups, matters. Under districts, specify a within-district or pooled association. A belief that both parties mobilise where one party is stronger is stronger than a sign on a weighted sum of covariances.

Keep CR prominent as a **sensitivity comparison**. For a continuous two-group single-column completion, M3 explains why the realised CR branch can agree with the margins even when its conclusion is wrong. Show the unsigned bound, the CR bound, and the data or historical argument supporting reinforcement. PHASE5a §2.2's optimal-face criterion is a better measure of what a sign buys for the user's target than the frequency of success in simulations generated under correct signs. A design using three groups can reach more independent directions; a third outcome can still be decisive when observations or sign beliefs link columns. Neither dimension count ranks the two designs for a given estimand.

### 6. Promote observations that directly constrain the target

Start with E5 on a common electorate and exact margins. E4 then addresses transfer to areas without a table under a shared or bounded-difference $K$. A published electorate table is an equality on the *actual* constituent cells. Its use as an equation for their expectations requires the residual allowance already acknowledged in Avenues §13.1. Under the mean model, one compatible table contributes **at most** $(R-1)(C-1)$ independent linear equations. Rank depends on which groups coexist. For a scalar target $a^\top k$ and table map $Ak=b$, $a$ in the row space of $A$ suffices for point identification even when $k$ remains underidentified. Boundary restrictions can identify further targets. Report target-specific residual width and conditioning of the stacked maps. Full rank alone gives no measure of precision.

Avenues §3.2 C1 establishes a negative result for a single contest's marginal cell. It leaves room for large gains on **joint targets** from ballot records (Part II, idea 4). The shock result in Avenues §7 also has a scope: with additive zero-mean shocks independent of composition, the projected shock law has no $K$ term. Support restrictions on latent probabilities may still trim $K$, as §7 itself notes. Point identification by deconvolution would require stronger information or assumptions. These qualifications change which data a project should seek.

### 7. Use integrality where it can change the decision

With integer row and column margins, a standard transportation polytope has integer vertices. Products of those polytopes therefore deliver exact integer **endpoints** for any linear count target with known denominators. Intermediate scalar values can still have gaps. Extra sign, cross-unit or overlapping-margin rows can destroy this property. A positive LP feasibility margin is insufficient to prove an integer witness exists. Conversely, infeasibility of the relaxation rules out integer completions too.

Offer an optional integer check for a specified branch, endpoint, or refutation claim whenever the data are exact counts and the added constraints lack an integrality certificate. Return an integer witness or a solver certificate; if computation stops early, return the relaxation and the unresolved gap. Trigger the check by the question, the additional rows and the decision margin. A rare group in a large precinct may matter more than a small precinct with a homogeneous electorate. Conditional expectations are real-valued even when counts are integers. For margins alone any fractional conditional allocation is a mixture of integer tables; restrictions that must hold in each realised allocation belong inside that mixture.

### 8. Keep the descriptive DiD and the causal effect visible together

PHASE2d §§0, 8, 10 make a valuable distinction that Design §7 blurs by labelling ordering of changes “MTR.” The observable-world inequality $\Delta m_v\geq\Delta m_n$ concerns changes in two groups. Causal monotone treatment response compares the same group's outcomes under service and under no service. A positive veteran-minus-nonveteran change can coexist with a harmful effect if future veterans would otherwise have improved even more.

For a stayer population with a well-defined untreated comparison and no relevant interference, let $D$ be the descriptive DiD and let $b$ be the difference in untreated group trends. Then $ATT=D-b$. A rectangular assumption $|b|\le M$ with freely attainable $b$ gives the familiar expansion, subject to feasible counterfactual rates. With multiple pre-periods, apply relative-magnitude or smoothness constraints to **one feasible sequence** of latent pre-trends and the post-period violation. Optimise the ATT over that joint set. Picking the most convenient endpoint of each pre-period interval independently can imply incompatible histories. Spillovers onto non-veterans need a separate exposure contrast or an explicit sensitivity term. For Wisconsin, establish common eligibility, abstention, service timing and bounds on entry and exit before giving the resulting ATT a causal label. A wide pre-period placebo containing zero has little evidentiary force. [Rambachan and Roth (2023)](https://economics.mit.edu/research/publications/more-credible-approach-parallel-trends)

## Part II · Research avenues, ordered by priority

### 1. Exact subset inequalities for the first M-SHARP release

1. **Idea.** At a fixed $v=(x,n)$ with two groups, write $x$ for the share of group 1. Its realised masses across choices are $y_c=x\beta_{1c}$, with $0\le y_c\le T_c$ and $\sum_c y_c=x$. For any subset $A$ of outcomes, put $T(A)=\sum_{c\in A}T_c$. Then the exact conditional mean mass set is described by
   $$
   \bar y(\mathcal C)=x,\qquad
   \bar y(A)\le E[\min\{x,T(A)\}\mid v]\quad(\varnothing\ne A\subsetneq\mathcal C).
   $$
   Complementary sets give the lower bounds. Here $\bar y=E[y\mid v]$ and $\mathcal C$ is the full set of outcome columns. The other group's mean masses are $E[T_c\mid v]-\bar y_c$. **Derivation.** To maximise a linear score, sort outcomes by their score and fill capacities in that order. At every prefix $A$ the cumulative mass equals $\min(x,T(A))$. The same ordering maximises at every return $T$, so the expected greedy allocation attains the expected support value in every direction. The subset inequalities bound every directional score by that same value, by writing the score as a constant plus a nonnegative sum of prefix scores. Equality of support functions gives equality of compact convex sets. For **R×2**, positive-outcome masses have capacities $x_r$ and random total $T$. Replace the upper bound by $E[\min\{\sum_{r\in A}x_r,T\}\mid v]$ and the total by $E[T\mid v]$. This proves a special case of the finite-direction conjecture in Avenues §6 and identifies the directions explicitly.
2. **Information or assumption.** Use the conditional law of observed returns with exact unit margins. Apply the restrictions to a proposed conditional mean at the original conditioning level. Under size-invariant LCE, the same $m(x)$ must work at every supported size. Use mass coordinates at structural zeros, where the absent group's rate has no realised weight. No vote-independence, deconvolution or shape assumption is required for this geometry.
3. **Expected identifying power — medium, sometimes large.** For 2×3 or 3×2, singletons and their complements exhaust the proper subsets. For 2×4, take $x=1/2$ and let $T$ be equally likely to be $(1/2,1/2,0,0)$ or $(0,0,1/2,1/2)$. The candidate $\bar y=(1/4,1/4,0,0)$ satisfies the individual cell limits and both mean margins. Yet the first two columns together can receive only $1/4$ in expectation, and the candidate assigns $1/2$. In fact their combined first-row mass is exactly $1/4$. This is a population-information gain; finite-sample bands can erase it. I separately compared the formula with direct conditional transportation LPs in 96 small random finite-law examples (both orientations, 2–5 categories). Largest support-value difference was $2.3\times10^{-16}$ or less.
4. **Fit.** The population inequalities are LP rows in the supported mean masses, hence in $k$ under LCE with $Q$ known. Enforce aggregate-mean equalities as well. For general R×C and known finite conditional support $t_j$ with probabilities $p_j$, use one feasible table $b_j\in S(x,t_j)$ per atom and $m=\sum_jp_j b_j$. This lifted LP gives an exact reference without a general finite-direction theorem. Unit-level constraints must alter the feasible $b_j$ before averaging; mean-level signs act afterward.
5. **Estimability and inference.** Each right-hand side is the expectation of an observable bounded response. A direct confidence procedure is available even with sparse $(X,N)$: for each prespecified nonnegative design instrument $g_j(v_i)$ average the unit inequalities with fixed weights $a_{ji}\ge0$, $\sum_i a_{ji}=1$. If $f_{ji}(H)$ is a candidate left-hand side and $Z_{ji}\in[0,1]$ its observed upper bound, impose
   $$
   \sum_i a_{ji} f_{ji}(H)\le \sum_i a_{ji}Z_{ji}
       +\sqrt{\tfrac12\log(J/\alpha)\sum_i a_{ji}^2}.
   $$
   Conditional independence in D3 and Hoeffding's inequality give simultaneous coverage for $J$ one-sided inequalities. Count both signs of noisy equalities in $J$, and fit $Q,K$ jointly or allocate error jointly if estimating $Q$ separately. No assumption of constant rates inside a bin is needed: each left side is evaluated at its own design. This construction can equally use nonparametric mean values at supported types as its unknowns. It covers the model parameters and their fixed-design mean target. An interval for a realised rate still needs a jointly budgeted residual allowance. A finite instrument family gives an outer confidence procedure; refinement needs enough mass per instrument and a proof of approximation to the conditional identified set. Replicated strata are the first benchmark, followed by sparse-size strata where power may vanish. Andrews–Shi offers an asymptotic alternative subject to its own design conditions.
6. **Key references.** Edmonds (1970, *Combinatorial Structures and Their Applications*) for greedy optimisation; Beresteanu, Molchanov and Molinari (2011, *Econometrica*) for convex random sets; Andrews and Shi (2013, *Econometrica*) and Hoeffding (1963, *JASA*) for inference. The EI subset formulas and proposed implementation are derived here.
7. **Priority — high.** It gives R9 a precise first deliverable, a sharp benchmark and a transparent failure case for coordinate-only smoothing.

### 2. Choose validation tables by how much they resolve the target

1. **Idea.** Before spending money on individual records, compute the guaranteed change in the chosen target's DD width from fully recovering each candidate precinct. Write the target as $\sum_i c_i^\top n_i$ with known denominators absorbed into $c_i$. On the product of precinct polytopes $S_i$, its width is exactly $\sum_i d_i$, where $d_i=\max_{S_i}c_i^\top n_i-\min_{S_i}c_i^\top n_i$. Recovering precinct $i$ removes precisely $d_i$ whatever its true table is. At equal costs, take the largest $d_i$; at unequal fixed costs, solve a 0–1 knapsack. This adds a prospective design step to E5/E6.
2. **Information or assumption.** Exact margins and the cost of recovering a complete table for the same people suffice. The identities, linkage and outcomes must truly be recoverable. A poll book can establish participation while leaving a secret vote unknown. Price the observation an archive can actually yield; treat probabilistic linkage or partial recovery as a different experiment.
3. **Expected identifying power — large when uncertainty is concentrated.** If three feasible precincts contribute 2, 18 and 7 percentage points to a group-rate interval, one equal-cost audit of the second removes 18 of 27 points. A precinct dominated by the focal group can be almost entirely uninformative to audit despite containing many of its voters. With only individual sampling, the gain depends on what is drawn.
4. **Fit.** Exact recovered counts are rows on actual cells, compatible with DD, realised signs, and integer checks. Under shared restrictions across precincts, baseline widths no longer add: condition the whole LP on each possible audit result and compare *worst remaining width* or a declared expected-width criterion. For exact mean-table transfers, inspect how the table map cuts the two target-optimal faces (PHASE5a §2.2). Allow for the actual-versus-mean discrepancy in E4.
5. **Estimability and inference.** Targeted complete recovery only constrains the audited units; no random selection is required for the exact DD gain. Extrapolation from a convenience sample to unaudited precincts needs a transfer or selection model. A probability sample of people instead permits design-based joint confidence constraints, with survey weights, nonresponse and reporting error accounted for. Validate the gain calculation by hiding and then restoring known NZ tables. Compare with largest-precinct and random-budget designs on the same targets.
6. **Key references.** Glynn and Wakefield (2010, *Statistical Methodology*) on hybrid EI; Särndal, Swensson and Wretman (1992, Springer) on survey designs. The additive audit-width criterion follows from separability.
7. **Priority — high.** It changes a fieldwork decision today and gives users an interpretable payoff for collecting information.

### 3. A finite-difference sandwich for observed composition changes

1. **Idea.** For a single outcome, compare two supported compositions $x,z$ in comparable contexts. Write $m_x,m_z$ for vectors of row means, $\tau_x=x^\top m_x$, and use bars for the arithmetic averages of the two vectors. The exact symmetric decomposition is
   $$
   \tau_z-\tau_x=(z-x)^\top\bar m+\bar x^\top(m_z-m_x).
   $$
   The first term measures replacing one mix of groups by another at the average rates. The second measures changes in the groups' rates at the average mix. For $z-x=t(e_r-e_s)$, $t>0$, a positive average $r$-versus-$s$ gap and positive weighted contextual change confine **each** term to $[0,\tau_z-\tau_x]$. This extends Design §5.1 to secants on supported pairs. Both terms remain linear in unknown means. Under LCE the mix term equals $t$ times the midpoint group gap and the rate term equals $t$ times the midpoint weighted contextual derivative.
2. **Information or assumption.** The identity uses observed composition and conditional return means. The directions are assumptions on the chosen pair of contexts. A researcher can defend a comparison between two turnout environments even when a slope at each intermediate composition is hard to defend. Hold district and size fixed, or explicitly state standardisation or size invariance. A negative aggregate change contradicts the two positive signs jointly. A positive change does little to validate them.
3. **Expected identifying power — medium.** At $x=.25$, $z=.75$ and mean returns $.45,.55$, both positive terms are at most $.10$. Hence the average between-group gap is in $[0,.20]$, although a positive sign alone supplies no such upper cap. Flat returns force both terms to zero if both are nonnegative. These are restrictions on pairs; reversals inside the interval can remain possible.
4. **Fit.** Two rows on supported mean vectors per comparison, or on $k$ under LCE. Intersects with idea 1, a finite-support N2 model or a district model. In R≥3, exact one-for-one exchange pairs may be scarce. A general observed displacement still has an interpretable mix term; attributing it to one chosen pair would add assumptions. Prespecify which comparisons are commensurate.
5. **Estimability and inference.** Begin with repeated types or matched contexts where endpoints have meaningful support. Estimate both conditional means jointly with uncertainty on the difference. For bins with varying compositions, the decomposition is exact for coherent stratum-level group masses and row-specific rates; the signs at that scale need a substantive interpretation. Sign transfer from all original pairs to two aggregated bins needs proof. Sparse comparisons should remain unreported or explicitly model-based.
6. **Key references.** Kitagawa (1955, *JASA*) for the symmetric rate decomposition; Manski and Pepper (2000, *Econometrica*) for ordering means across populations; Elzayn et al. (2025, NBER Working Paper 34285) for the EI sign interpretation. Using signs on these finite components is the proposed extension.
7. **Priority — high.** It keeps the scientific content of M-LOC available when local derivatives are unsupported, and gives the nonparametric core a concrete first shape module.

### 4. Use actual ballot patterns to bound group-specific differences across contests

1. **Idea.** For two binary contests let the observed counts of ballot patterns be $n_{00},n_{01},n_{10},n_{11}$ within each precinct. Treat patterns as columns of a group-by-pattern table. A group's support difference, contest 1 minus contest 2, equals its count in pattern 10 minus its count in pattern 01, divided by its size. Optimise this linear contrast directly. Avenues §12.3(d) studies *assumed* nesting; here the number and direction of discordant ballots are measured.
2. **Information or assumption.** Require actual joint contest totals and matching group margins for the same precinct ballot universe. Include blanks, roll-off and different contest eligibility as explicit categories where necessary. No common contextual coefficient or conditional independence of contest choices is required. Group turnout denominators may require extra margins if the desired population includes nonvoters.
3. **Expected identifying power — large for contrasts; point in the agreement limit.** In one precinct with 100 voters, 50 in each group, suppose 50 ballots are 00 and 50 are 11. Either group's support in either contest can range from 0 to 1. Its two support rates must nonetheless be equal: the group-specific difference is exactly zero. With at most four discordant ballots, its absolute difference is at most $4/50=.08$. The one-contest impossibility theorem survives intact.
4. **Fit.** A standard transportation LP on richer columns, with integer vertices. An optional sign on a contest margin is a row on sums of pattern cells. Several contests increase the number of observed patterns; aggregate patterns that have identical target coefficients and identical roles in every constraint.
5. **Estimability and inference.** Complete CVRs yield arithmetic bounds. Reconcile ballot-style coverage and precinct identifiers against official totals first. If only some ballots have joint records, include an unknown-pattern block for the rest. Claims about nonreporting jurisdictions would need additional transfer assumptions.
6. **Key references.** Kuriwaki et al. (2024, *Scientific Data*) provide the records; Cho and Manski (2008, *The Oxford Handbook of Political Methodology*) provide the cross-level perspective. The example is a direct count argument. [Public CVR data](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/PQQ3KV)
7. **Priority — medium, high for split-ticket applications.** It is a strict gain from richer observations before any M-MULTI behavioural modelling.

### 5. Allow exceptions to an ordering and report a joint breakdown frontier

1. **Idea.** Replace “the ordering holds in every precinct” by “at most a fraction $\pi$ of the electorate lives in precincts where the ordering fails by more than $s$.” For a realised rate gap $g_i\in[-1,1]$, impose $g_i\ge -s-z_i$, $z_i\in\{0,1\}$, $\sum_i p_i z_i\le\pi$, with $p_i=N_i/\sum_j N_j$ and $0\le s\le1$. For a claim such as a positive provincial gap, plot the boundary where the lower target endpoint hits zero in $(\pi,s)$. ASSUMPTION_MENU E14 already asks for frontiers. The new component is the exception-mass restriction and its exact finite-population implementation.
2. **Information or assumption.** A substantive belief about the prevalence of exceptional environments can be easier to defend than a universal maximum violation. Choose voter, focal-group or precinct weights to match that belief, and define eligibility for a within-precinct comparison when a group is absent. Large unknown violations can concentrate in the most influential precincts. Observable margins may imply a minimum exception mass; they do not establish that this minimum is the true prevalence.
3. **Expected identifying power — medium.** At $(0,0)$ this is unit ordering. At $\pi=1$ it permits every table. Between them a few large mixed units can determine whether a positive gap survives. In contrast, a uniform five-point relaxation cannot represent one strongly reversed small precinct alongside many correctly ordered ones.
4. **Fit.** Mixed-integer LP, with continuous or integer cell counts chosen separately. An easier convex alternative bounds $\sum_i p_i(-g_i)_+$ directly, using auxiliary slacks; that controls average severity and represents a different belief. With finite units the prevalence frontier can have jumps.
5. **Estimability and inference.** The frontier for exact margins and realised restrictions is deterministic. Include sampling uncertainty for survey margins or estimated means with one joint confidence region projected over the dial grid. Show the region in which the assumptions are infeasible separately from the region where every feasible table supports the conclusion. Pointwise confidence intervals across a searched grid do not establish a simultaneous frontier.
6. **Key references.** Masten and Poirier (2020, *Quantitative Economics*) for breakdown frontiers; Horowitz and Manski (1995, *Econometrica*) for partial identification under bounded contamination. Their inference theorems require separate verification for this discrete frontier.
7. **Priority — medium.** More informative for substantive users than further rankings of hard signs by their simulated width reductions.

### 6. Bound error in group classifications used to construct the margins

1. **Idea.** In historical or linked-record data the group label can be uncertain. Introduce counts $n_{i,r,\ell,c}$ by true group $r$, recorded label $\ell$ and outcome $c$. Enforce the observed recorded-label totals and outcome totals. Add audited upper bounds on counts of false matches and missed matches, separately when evidence permits. Project onto the target defined by true group. This extends E5/E6 to errors in the composition margin itself.
2. **Information or assumption.** Distinguish record linkage error, missing eligible people, and outcome misreporting. A clerical audit or two validated registers can supply bounds on the first; completeness of the population base is a separate assumption. Allow errors to depend on the outcome unless evidence justifies independence. A global audit error rate transfers to a high-error district only under an explicit allocation rule or a pooled error budget.
3. **Expected identifying power — medium relative to unknown group labels.** If a precinct has 20 recorded veterans and the count of incorrect labels is bounded by two people, an assignment that changes ten veterans' labels is ruled out. A two-person error may still move a rare-group support rate substantially. Relative to treating every label as correct this procedure properly widens the bounds; audit evidence then narrows them.
4. **Fit.** Linear count restrictions. A single rate with unknown positive denominator can be bounded by linear-fractional optimisation or threshold feasibility. A difference of two ratios with unknown denominators needs joint treatment, for example profiling over feasible integer group totals. Plugging the unknown true composition into an LCE polynomial generally breaks the simple LP. Start at the accounting rung.
5. **Estimability and inference.** Use exact audit counts within audited records. For transport to a target population, construct a design-valid joint region for error counts or rates, including nonresponse. Project it jointly with the ecological margins. Show how bounds vary under outcome-dependent linkage, especially in veteran or historical census applications.
6. **Key references.** Molinari (2008, *Journal of Econometrics*) on linear restrictions for misclassification; Särndal, Swensson and Wretman (1992, Springer) on audit sampling.
7. **Priority — medium, conditional on a real audit.** This protects the meaning of the group in precisely the applications in which a narrow LCE interval would otherwise look most persuasive.

### Which lower-ranked avenues deserve another look?

- **Revive transport and capacity methods as computation.** Avenues §7 labels spatial structure I and combines entropy, OT and Choquet in J; Design §4.1 groups the labels differently. The substantive recommendation applies under either numbering. Idea 1 turns capacity inequalities into exact, interpretable rows. Idea 4 supplies an observed transport margin. An entropy-maximising completion would select one possible table without gaining this information.
- **Keep label symmetry (G) as an application-specific sensitivity check.** Symmetry of a ballot design does not establish symmetry of voters' responses. A known symmetric randomisation of labels would change that argument; without one, compare endpoints with and without the restriction.
- **Keep additive-shock deconvolution (H) low.** The kernel calculation in §7 explains why recovering the distribution of projected shocks cannot by itself assign contextual behaviour to groups. The residual support channel is real. Estimating its endpoints accurately enough to improve bounds is a poor near-term bet.
- **Spatial and hierarchical borrowing need an explicit premise.** Sharing a conditional mean, bounding differences between specified neighbouring areas, and smoothing a noisy estimate give three different guarantees. Avenues §12.3(f) already considers all-pairs similarity. Revisit spatial ordering through supported comparisons or audited neighbours before developing a general spatial prior. Treat A1/A2 dispersion, tensor-factor contest models, large polynomial/SOS hierarchies and robust-Bayes summaries as research options. Each needs either substantial extra modelling, rich data, or a user demand for a distribution over values that the margins leave open.
